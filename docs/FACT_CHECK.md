# MOON

## Surveyor 3: Final Card Wording

Chapter 4 · Moon. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Surveyor 3.

### Intro

A robotic lander launched on 17 April 1967 and landed on 20 April 1967. It sent back 6,326 TV pictures and used a surface sampler, which could reach up to 5 feet (1.5 m) and dig up to 7 inches (18 cm) deep, to dig four trenches, run four bearing tests and perform thirteen impact tests.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Surveyor 3

- **Years:** 1967

- **Site:** Southeastern Oceanus Procellarum

- **Coordinates:** 3° S, 23.41° W

### Why it was left

Surveyor 3 was an uncrewed lander. Its last contact was on 4 May 1967, two days after the lunar night began, and it stayed where it landed. NASA's page does not say why contact ended.

### The landing

Its landing was rough. Highly reflective rocks confused the descent radar, and the main engine did not cut off at the right moment, about 14 feet (4.3 m) above the surface. The lander bounced twice, first to about 33 feet (10 m) and then to about 10 feet (3 m), before it settled on the third touchdown.

### The science it enabled

The soil behaved like wet sand, with a bearing strength of about 10 psi (98 kPa), so scientists judged the surface solid enough to support an Apollo Lunar Module. On 18 November 1969, Apollo 12 landed about 180 m (197 yards) away. During their second moonwalk on 19 November, the astronauts recovered parts, including the soil scoop and camera system, so scientists could study the effects of nearly two and a half years on the Moon.

### Where it is now

Surveyor 3 is inactive at its landing site in southeastern Oceanus Procellarum (3° S, 23.41° W). NASA's page does not describe its present condition. Apollo 12 astronauts removed some parts in 1969.

## Apollo 11 Lunar Module Eagle (descent stage): Final Card Wording

Chapter 1 · Moon. Fact-checked against NASA NSSDCA: Apollo 11 Lunar Module / EASEP (1969-059C).

### Intro

Eagle was the two-stage lunar module that carried Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin to the Moon's surface. Its descent stage, which you found, is what remains at the landing site.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Apollo 11

- **Years:** 1969

- **Site:** Sea of Tranquility (Mare Tranquilitatis)

- **Coordinates:** 0.67° N, 23.47° E

### Why it was left

The descent stage was the launch platform for the ascent stage, so it was left behind on the Moon when the ascent stage lifted off on 21 July 1969. The ascent stage docked with the Command/Service Module and was then jettisoned into lunar orbit on 22 July (UT). NASA says the fate of the ascent stage is not known, but it is assumed to have crashed into the Moon within the following 1 to 4 months.

### The science it enabled

Eagle carried the Early Apollo Scientific Experiment Package (EASEP), a 48 kg unit of self-contained instruments with solar panels for power. It included a passive seismometer, designed to measure seismic activity and the physical properties of the Moon's crust and interior, and a lunar dust detector, designed to measure dust buildup and radiation damage to solar cells. A laser ranging retroreflector also counted as part of the EASEP, but it was not attached to the unit, needed no power, and was set up about 5 m away.

The EASEP was turned on by ground command on 21 July 1969 and worked even though its temperatures ran 30 °C above the planned maximum. It was switched off before lunar sunset on 3 August as its power dropped, as predicted, and was turned on again the next lunar day. On 27 August 1969, near noon of that second lunar day, it stopped accepting commands.

### Where it is now

The descent stage is at 0.67416° N, 23.47314° E in Mare Tranquilitatis, a position NASA determined from Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter images. NASA's catalog page does not describe its present condition.

## Apollo 15 Lunar Module Falcon, ALSEP and rover: Final Card Wording

Chapter 2 · Moon. Fact-checked against NASA NSSDCA: Apollo 15 Lunar Module / ALSEP (1971-063C).

### Intro

Falcon was Apollo 15's two-stage lunar module. David Scott and James Irwin landed in it at the foot of the Apennine Mountains. Over three moonwalks totaling 18 hours 35 minutes, the crew covered 27.9 km and collected 77.31 kg of rock and soil samples. They drove the Lunar Roving Vehicle on all three traverses.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Apollo 15

- **Years:** 1971

- **Site:** Mare Imbrium region, at the foot of the Apennine Mountains

- **Coordinates:** 26.13° N, 3.63° E

### Why it was left

The descent stage was left on the Moon as the launch platform for the ascent stage. The ascent stage lifted off on 2 August 1971, docked with the Command/Service Module, and was jettisoned on 3 August (UT). The ALSEP stayed behind to keep recording until it was shut down on 30 September 1977. The Lunar Roving Vehicle was also left on the Moon.

### The science it enabled

The ALSEP had seven instruments, all designed to measure different things on the Moon:

- **Passive seismometer:** seismic activity and the physical properties of the crust and interior.

- **Lunar surface magnetometer:** the magnetic field at the surface.

- **Solar wind spectrometer:** the electrons and protons that stream from the Sun to the Moon.

- **Suprathermal ion detector:** the flux, composition, energy and velocity of low-energy positive ions.

- **Cold cathode ion gauge:** the lunar atmosphere and any changes with time or solar activity.

- **Lunar dust detector:** dust buildup, radiation damage to solar cells, and temperatures.

- **Heat flow experiment:** the rate of heat loss from the Moon's interior and the thermal properties of lunar material.

A SNAP-27 radioisotope thermoelectric generator powered the package. It produced about 70 W, and 63.5 W after one year. The central station was turned on at 18:37 UT on 31 July 1971.

### Where it is now

The descent stage is at 26.13239° N, 3.63330° E. The ALSEP central station nearby (26.1341° N, 3.6298° E) was shut down on 30 September 1977. The ascent stage was jettisoned and crashed into the Moon at 26.36° N, 0.25° E. NASA's catalog page does not describe the present condition of the descent stage or the ALSEP.

## Apollo 12 Lunar Module Intrepid and ALSEP: Final Card Wording

Chapter 3 · Moon. Fact-checked against NASA NSSDCA: Apollo 12 Lunar Module / ALSEP (1969-099C).

### Intro

Intrepid carried Pete Conrad and Alan Bean to Oceanus Procellarum, the Ocean of Storms. It landed on 19 November 1969, within about 180 m of the Surveyor 3 lander.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Apollo 12

- **Years:** 1969

- **Site:** Oceanus Procellarum (Ocean of Storms)

- **Coordinates:** 3.01° S, 23.42° W

### Why it was left

The descent stage was left on the Moon as the launch platform for the ascent stage, which lifted off on 20 November 1969 and docked with the Command/Service Module. The ascent stage was then jettisoned and deliberately crashed into the Moon about 60 km from the Apollo 12 seismic station, creating the first recorded artificial moonquake. The ALSEP stayed behind to keep recording.

### The science it enabled

The astronauts set up an ALSEP with six instruments, each designed to measure something different on the Moon:

- **Passive seismometer:** seismic activity and the physical properties of the crust and interior.

- **Suprathermal ion detector:** the flux, composition, energy and velocity of low-energy positive ions.

- **Cold cathode ion gauge:** the lunar atmosphere and any changes with time or solar activity.

- **Lunar dust detector:** dust buildup, radiation damage to solar cells, and temperatures.

- **Lunar surface magnetometer:** the magnetic field at the surface.

- **Solar wind spectrometer:** the electrons and protons that stream from the Sun to the Moon.

A SNAP-27 radioisotope thermoelectric generator powered the package. The crew also collected about 34.35 kg of rock and soil, a solar wind foil, and about 10 kg of parts from Surveyor 3.

### Where it is now

The descent stage is at 3.0128° S, 23.4219° W. The ALSEP central station (3.0094° S, 23.4246° W) was turned on on 19 November 1969 and was shut down, with all the ALSEP stations, on 30 September 1977. The ascent stage struck the Moon at 3.94° S, 338.80° E. NASA's catalog page does not describe the present condition of the descent stage or the ALSEP.

## Sojourner rover and Pathfinder lander: Final Card Wording

Chapter 5 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Mars Pathfinder and NASA GISS Mars24: Mars Lander Missions.

### Intro

Sojourner was a 10.6 kg (23 lb) rover, the first robotic rover delivered to the surface of Mars. It rolled down the rear ramp of the Pathfinder lander, named the Carl Sagan Memorial Station, late on sol 2 (the night of 5 July 1997).

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Mars Pathfinder

- **Years:** 1997

- **Site:** Ares Vallis

- **Coordinates:** 19.47° N, 326.75° E (the same as 33.25° W)

### Why it was left

Pathfinder landed at Ares Vallis on 4 July 1997. NASA's mission page gives 27 September 1997 as the end of the mission, the date of the final data transmission. NASA's Mars24 page lists the last transmission received as sol 93 (7 October 1997). Neither NASA page gives a reason for the end of operations.

### The science it enabled

Pathfinder's instruments included an Alpha Proton X-ray Spectrometer, three cameras and an atmospheric structure and weather package. Rounded pebbles and cobbles at the landing site suggested conglomerates formed in running water, which points to a warmer past in which liquid water was stable. The mission also found magnetic airborne dust, dust devils and early morning water-ice clouds.

From landing until the final data transmission, the mission returned 2.3 billion bits of data, including more than 16,500 images from the lander, 550 images from the rover, and more than 15 chemical analyses of rocks and soil.

### Where it is now

The Pathfinder lander touched down at 19.47° N, 326.75° E in Ares Vallis, an ancient flood plain in Mars's northern hemisphere. Sojourner traveled about 100 meters during the mission. NASA's pages do not describe the present condition of the lander or the rover.

## Spirit rover: Final Card Wording

Chapter 6 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Mars Exploration Rover Spirit and NASA 3D Resources.

### Intro

Spirit was one of NASA's twin Mars Exploration Rovers, with Opportunity. It launched on 10 June 2003 and landed on 3 January 2004 in Gusev Crater, at 14.5692° S, 175.4729° E, about 13.4 km from its planned target. The site was named Columbia Memorial Station.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Mars Exploration Rovers (Spirit)

- **Years:** 2004–2011

- **Site:** Gusev Crater

- **Coordinates:** 14.57° S, 175.47° E (landing site)

### Why it was left

On 1 May 2009, Spirit became stuck in soft soil while driving south beside the western edge of a low plateau called Home Plate. On 28 November 2009, its right rear wheel stopped working. After 22 March 2010, controllers could not regain contact. More than 1,300 commands were sent without success. NASA says the most probable cause was excessive cold that made its survival heaters ineffective. NASA ended its recovery efforts and declared the mission complete on 25 May 2011.

### The science it enabled

In 2004, Spirit found a surprising variety of bedrock in the Columbia Hills, and some of the rocks showed signs of alteration by water. In 2005, it filmed dust devils in motion. In 2006, it found evidence of an ancient explosion at Home Plate. In 2007, it unearthed a patch of nearly pure silica, which NASA says is strong evidence that ancient Mars was much wetter than it is now.

Spirit drove 4.8 miles (7.73 km) and worked for 6 years, 2 months and 19 days, more than 25 times its intended lifetime.

### Where it is now

Spirit is no longer operating. NASA says the rover was silent after March 2010. It became stuck beside Home Plate in Gusev Crater in 2009, and efforts to free it failed. NASA's page gives its landing coordinates (14.5692° S, 175.4729° E), not the position where it became stuck.

### 3D model

NASA 3D Resources, Mars Exploration Rover (Opportunity, MER-B), used here for its twin, Spirit. NASA's own Spirit page shows a 3D model described as depicting both twin rovers.

### Sources

## Opportunity rover: Final Card Wording

Chapter 7 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Mars Exploration Rover Opportunity and NASA 3D Resources.

### Intro

Opportunity was one of NASA's twin Mars Exploration Rovers, with Spirit. It launched on 7 July 2003 and landed in January 2004 on Meridiani Planum, at 1.9483° S, 354.47417° E, about 14.9 km from its intended target. It came down inside Eagle crater. (NASA's page gives both 24 and 25 January for the landing.)

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Mars Exploration Rovers (Opportunity)

- **Years:** 2004–2018 (last signal 10 June 2018; mission declared complete 13 February 2019)

- **Site:** Meridiani Planum

- **Coordinates:** 1.95° S, 354.47° E (the same as 5.53° W)

### Why it was left

A severe Mars-wide dust storm blanketed the rover's location in June 2018 and reduced the energy available to its solar panels. The last signal from Opportunity was heard on 10 June 2018, and NASA declared the mission complete on 13 February 2019.

### The science it enabled

Opportunity found evidence that Mars may once have been able to sustain microbial life. Near its discarded heat shield, it found an iron-nickel meteorite in January 2005. It explored Victoria Crater and Endeavour Crater, and it drove through Marathon Valley.

It drove 28.06 miles (45.16 km) in total. On 25 March 2015, NASA announced that Opportunity had passed the marathon distance (26.219 miles, 42.195 km), which NASA calls the first human enterprise to do so on another world. Its total was an off-world distance record until NASA's Perseverance rover passed it on 11 October 2026.

### Where it is now

Opportunity is no longer operating. NASA describes its final resting spot as Perseverance Valley, where the rover had traveled more than 28 miles (45 km) by the time it stopped.

## Viking 1 lander: Final Card Wording

Chapter 8 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Viking 1, NASA 3D Resources: Viking Lander, and NASA GISS Mars24.

### Intro

Viking 1 landed on 20 July 1976 on the western slope of Chryse Planitia, about 28 km from its planned target. It sent back high-quality color photographs and a 300-degree panorama, and recorded temperatures from about −86 °C before dawn to about −33 °C in the afternoon.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Viking 1

- **Years:** 1976–1982

- **Site:** Chryse Planitia

- **Coordinates:** 22.48° N, 47.94° W

### Why it was left

The lander stopped operating on 11 November 1982, when a faulty command sent from Earth interrupted communications. Further attempts to regain contact were unsuccessful. It had operated for just over six years after landing.

### The science it enabled

On 28 July 1976, the lander's robot arm scooped up the first soil samples and placed them in its biological laboratory, which included a gas chromatograph mass spectrometer. The combined data from four samples could have been read as a weak sign of life, but the major test for organic compounds gave negative results. The data also showed an abundance of sulfur. The lander's seismometer was inoperable.

### Where it is now

The lander is no longer operating. It set down at 22.483° N, 47.94° W. In January 1982, it was renamed the Thomas Mutch Memorial Station, in honor of Thomas A. Mutch, the leader of the Viking imaging team. NASA's page does not describe the present condition of the lander.

## Viking 2 lander: Final Card Wording

Chapter 9 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Viking 2, NASA 3D Resources: Viking Lander, and NASA GISS Mars24.

### Intro

Viking 2 was a combined orbiter and lander mission to Mars, launched on 9 September 1975 from Cape Canaveral, Florida. Its lander touched down on 3 September 1976 in Utopia Planitia, about 6,460 km from the Viking 1 landing site, at 47.968° N, 225.71° W.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Viking 2

- **Years:** 1976–1980

- **Site:** Utopia Planitia

- **Coordinates:** 47.97° N, 225.71° W (the same as 134.29° E)

### Why it was left

The lander transmitted data until 12 April 1980, which NASA lists as the end of the lander mission. NASA's page does not say what ended its transmissions. The Viking 2 orbiter's operations ended earlier, on 24 July 1978, after a series of leaks.

### The science it enabled

The lander's soil biology experiments, with soil scooped up on three occasions beginning on 12 September 1976, gave results similar to Viking 1's: inconclusive on whether life exists or ever existed on the surface of Mars. Scientists believed the soil contained reactants, created by ultraviolet light, that could produce characteristics similar to those of living organisms in Earth soil.

Across the whole Viking program, the two orbiters returned 52,663 images of Mars and mapped about 97% of the surface at a resolution of about 300 meters (980 feet). The two landers returned about 4,500 photos of their landing sites.

### Where it is now

The lander is no longer operating and is in Utopia Planitia. NASA notes that, because the terrain had so few features to use as references, orbiter imagery could not precisely locate the lander, so its position is less certain than the coordinates suggest. In July 2001, the lander was renamed the Gerald Soffen Memorial Station, after Gerald Soffen, the NASA Project Scientist for Viking, who had died the year before. NASA's page does not describe the present condition of the lander.

### 3D model

NASA 3D Resources, Viking Lander. NASA's page for this model links it to Viking 1 and says two identical Viking spacecraft were built, so it also stands in for Viking 2.

### Sources

- NASA Science: Viking 2, https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viking-2/

- NASA 3D Resources: Viking Lander, https://science.nasa.gov/3d-resources/viking-lander/

## InSight lander: Final Card Wording

Chapter 10 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: InSight, NASA 3D Resources: InSight Cruise Lander, and NASA GISS Mars24.

### Intro

InSight launched on 5 May 2018 and landed on 26 November 2018 on the western side of Elysium Planitia. It was the first robotic explorer built to study the inside of Mars in depth: its crust, mantle and core. It carried a seismometer and a heat flow probe, which its robotic arm lifted from the deck and placed on the surface.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** InSight

- **Years:** 2018–2022

- **Site:** Elysium Planitia

- **Coordinates:** about 4.5° N, 135.9° E (the center of the landing site)

### Why it was left

NASA's mission page lists the end of the mission as 15 December 2022. NASA GISS's Mars24 page says 15 December was the day of the lander's last communication, and that the mission was declared effectively over on 21 December 2022. Dust building up on its solar panels had reduced the probe's ability to generate power by almost 95%. The mission page itself does not spell out a cause.

### The science it enabled

InSight was designed to take Mars's vital signs: its pulse, temperature and reflexes. Its marsquake data revealed a "lumpy" interior, with rocky material from ancient impacts scattered in giant lumps through the mantle. Its sensors also picked up a low rumble caused by vibrations from the Martian wind, which NASA estimates was blowing at 10 to 15 mph (5 to 7 meters a second) on 1 December 2018.

### Where it is now

The lander is retired. NASA says its landing site is in the western part of Elysium Planitia, centered at about 4.5° N, 135.9° E, about 600 km from the Curiosity rover's landing site in Gale Crater. NASA's mission page does not describe the present condition of the lander.

### 3D model

NASA 3D Resources, InSight Cruise Lander (arm deployed). NASA describes this model as the landed configuration, with solar panels and arm deployed, but before the seismometer and heat probe were placed on the ground.

## Ingenuity helicopter: Final Card Wording

Chapter 11 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Ingenuity Mars Helicopter, NASA's mission-end news release (25 January 2024), NASA 3D Resources: Ingenuity Mars Helicopter, and NASA GISS Mars24.

### Intro

Ingenuity was a small helicopter that flew at Jezero Crater, Mars. It made the first powered, controlled flight on another planet on 19 April 2021 and flew 72 times in total. It was built as a technology demonstration meant for no more than five test flights in 30 days.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Mars 2020 Perseverance (Ingenuity)

- **Years:** 2021–2024

- **Site:** Jezero Crater

- **Coordinates:** 18.44° N, 77.45° E (the Perseverance landing site, where Ingenuity was delivered)

### Why it was left

Ingenuity flew for the last time on 18 January 2024, and NASA declared its mission complete on 25 January 2024. The helicopter lost contact with the rover about 1 meter (3 feet) above the surface on that flight. Afterward, imagery showed that one or more rotor blades had been damaged, and NASA said it could no longer fly. NASA's release says the helicopter remained upright and in communication, and that the cause of the dropout and its orientation at touchdown were still being investigated.

### The science it enabled

On 19 April 2021, NASA's Ingenuity made the first powered, controlled flight on the Red Planet. After the technology demonstration, it served as an aerial scout for scientists and rover planners, and it also helped engineers study Perseverance's landing-gear debris. In its final phase, it ran experimental flight tests to learn the limits of its aerodynamics.

Over 72 flights it logged about 128.8 minutes in the air, covered about 17.0 km (10.5 miles) and reached as high as 24.0 m (78.7 feet).

### Where it is now

Ingenuity remains at Jezero Crater. NASA's mission page and news release do not give its final position as coordinates or describe its present condition. At the time of the mission-end release, NASA said the helicopter was upright and Perseverance was too far away to image it at its final airfield. The coordinates above are Perseverance's landing site from NASA's Mars24 page, not the helicopter's final position.

## Phoenix lander: Final Card Wording

Chapter 12 · Mars. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Mars Phoenix and NASA GISS Mars24: Mars Lander Missions.

### Intro

Phoenix was a NASA lander whose objective was to search for evidence of past or present microbial life. It launched on 4 August 2007 from Cape Canaveral, Florida, and landed on 25 May 2008 in the Green Valley of Vastitas Borealis, an arctic plain on Mars, at 68.22° N, 234.25° E. It landed farther north than any previous Mars mission, at a latitude equal to northern Alaska.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Mars Phoenix

- **Years:** 2008

- **Site:** Green Valley, Vastitas Borealis

- **Coordinates:** 68.22° N, 125.75° W (the same as 234.25° E)

### Why it was left

NASA's mission page lists 2 November 2008 as the end of the mission and does not state a cause. NASA's Mars24 page says the last communication came on that date, that the lander could no longer get enough sunlight for its solar panels as the seasons changed, and that later orbiter imagery suggests ice accumulated over the winter broke off its solar panels.

### The science it enabled

Phoenix verified water ice in the Martian subsurface. On 19 June 2008, scientists concluded that bright clumps in a trench dug by the robotic arm were probably water ice, because the material vaporized four days after it was exposed. On 31 July 2008, NASA announced there is water on Mars, based on a sample analysed by the lander's TEGA instrument.

It also found perchlorate in the soil, announced on 5 August 2008. NASA says the finding neither confirmed nor refuted the possibility of life, and that perchlorate is a possible energy source for microbes.

### Where it is now

Phoenix is no longer operating. NASA's pages give its landing coordinates (68.22° N, 234.25° E) but do not describe its present condition. NASA's Mars24 page says orbital imagery suggests its solar panels were broken off by ice.

# DEEP SPACE

## Dawn spacecraft: Final Card Wording

Chapter 13 · Deep space. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Dawn, NASA's mission-end release (1 November 2018), and NASA 3D Resources: Dawn.

### Intro

NASA's Dawn was an orbiter that made extended stays at two small worlds in the main asteroid belt: the giant asteroid Vesta and the dwarf planet Ceres. It launched on 27 September 2007 and used ion propulsion. In 2011 it became the first spacecraft to orbit a body in the region between Mars and Jupiter, and in 2015 it became the first to visit a dwarf planet.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Dawn

- **Years:** 2007–2018

- **Where:** In orbit around Ceres, main asteroid belt

### Why it was left

Dawn missed scheduled contacts with NASA's Deep Space Network on 31 October and 1 November 2018. Mission managers concluded it had run out of hydrazine, the fuel that controls its pointing, so it could no longer aim its antennas at Earth or turn its solar panels to the Sun. NASA's release of 1 November 2018 announced the end of the mission.

Because Ceres has conditions of interest to scientists who study the chemistry that leads to life, NASA follows strict planetary protection protocols for disposing of the spacecraft. NASA says Dawn will stay in orbit for at least 20 years, with more than 99 percent confidence of at least 50 years. Unlike Cassini, it did not end with a final plunge.

### The science it enabled

Dawn let scientists compare two planet-like worlds that evolved very differently, and showed that location mattered to how objects formed and evolved in the early Solar System. NASA says its results reinforced the idea that dwarf planets could have hosted oceans over a significant part of their history, and potentially still do.

### Where it is now

Dawn is in orbit around Ceres, where NASA says it will remain for decades. NASA's pages do not give the shape of that final orbit, so the circle drawn in the game is only illustrative.

## NEAR Shoemaker spacecraft: Final Card Wording

Chapter 14 · Deep space. Fact-checked against NASA Science: NEAR Shoemaker and NASA 3D Resources: NEAR Shoemaker.

### Intro

NASA calls NEAR the first spacecraft to orbit an asteroid, and it was also the first to land on one. It launched on 17 February 1996 from Cape Canaveral, Florida, and was renamed NEAR Shoemaker on 14 March 2000 to honor the geologist Eugene M. Shoemaker (1928–1997). Its target was the asteroid 433 Eros.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** NEAR Shoemaker

- **Years:** 1996–2001

- **Where:** On asteroid Eros, just south of the saddle-shaped feature Himeros

### Why it was left

NASA's page says the spacecraft was not designed as a lander, yet it survived a slow, controlled descent and touched down at about 4 mph (6.4 km/h) on 12 February 2001. It returned data for about two weeks. Its last contact was on 28 February 2001, when the spacecraft succumbed to extreme cold (about minus 173 °C). A contact attempt on 10 December 2002 was unsuccessful.

NASA's page does not say why the landing was attempted. It lists landing on Eros among the mission's objectives, and it also says the spacecraft was not designed as a lander, so the card states both and gives no reason.

### The science it enabled

NEAR found that Eros has no magnetic field. Its near-infrared spectrometer mapped more than 70 percent of the surface, and it returned about 10 times more data than planned, including 160,000 images. Eros turned out smaller than expected, with two medium-sized craters, a long surface ridge, and a density similar to Earth's crust.

### Where it is now

NEAR Shoemaker is on the surface of Eros, where it touched down on 12 February 2001 at 19:44 UT, just south of Himeros. NASA's page gives only this touchdown area, not exact coordinates, so the position drawn in the game on the asteroid's underside is illustrative.

## Pioneer 10 spacecraft: Final Card Wording

Chapter 15 · Deep space. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Pioneer 10 and NASA 3D Resources.

### Intro

Pioneer 10 was NASA's first mission to the outer planets, launched on 2 March 1972 from Cape Canaveral, Florida. NASA lists these firsts: the first spacecraft to fly beyond Mars, fly through the main asteroid belt and fly past Jupiter; the first human-made object to go beyond Neptune, crossing its orbit on 13 June 1983; the first placed on a trajectory to escape the Solar System; and the first to use all-nuclear electrical power. It was designed for a 21-month mission.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Pioneer 10

- **Years:** 1972–2003

- **Where:** Heading toward the star Aldebaran, in Taurus

### Why it was left

NASA ended routine contact with Pioneer 10 on 31 March 1997 for budgetary reasons. Intermittent contact continued while its onboard power allowed. Its nuclear power source then decayed until there was not enough power for the radio transmitter. The last signal was received on 23 January 2003 from about 12.23 billion km away, and it took 11 hours 20 minutes to arrive. A final contact attempt on 4 March 2006 failed.

### The science it enabled

Based on Pioneer 10's data, scientists identified plasma in Jupiter's magnetic field. It took about 500 images of Jupiter's atmosphere, including the Great Red Spot, and the first close-up images of Ganymede. In 1976, as it crossed Saturn's orbit, its data indicated that Jupiter's magnetic tail, almost 800 million km long, covered the whole distance between the two planets. On the way, it also measured zodiacal light and met fewer asteroid hits than expected.

### Where it is now

Pioneer 10 is heading out of the Solar System, generally in the direction of the red star Aldebaran, which NASA says it should pass in about two million years. NASA's latest figure is about 118.8 AU (roughly 17.7 billion km) from Earth on 5 November 2017. NASA's page does not give its current position, so the game shows it at that 2017 distance and does not extrapolate to today.

## Pioneer 11 spacecraft: Final Card Wording

Chapter 16 · Deep space. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Pioneer 11 and NASA 3D Resources.

### Intro

Pioneer 11 was the sister craft to Pioneer 10, launched on 6 April 1973 from Cape Canaveral, Florida. It flew past Jupiter on 3 December 1974, about 42,500 km above the cloud tops, and on 1 September 1979 it became the first spacecraft to study Saturn up close and the first human-made object to fly past it.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Pioneer 11

- **Years:** 1973–1995

- **Where:** Heading out of the Solar System, toward the center of the galaxy in the general direction of Sagittarius

### Why it was left

NASA's page says the mission ended in 1995. By then, two instruments were still working. NASA Ames made last contact on 30 September 1995, when the spacecraft was 44.1 AU from Earth. Scientists received a few minutes of good engineering data on 24 November 1995, and then lost contact again once Earth moved out of view of the spacecraft's antenna. NASA's page does not give a cause for the loss of contact.

### The science it enabled

At Jupiter it returned the first pictures of the planet's polar regions and about 200 images of its moons. At Saturn it took 440 images, discovered a narrow ring outside the A ring (the F ring) and a new moon about 200 km across, and gave the first conclusive evidence of Saturn's magnetic field. NASA says its data suggested Saturn is primarily liquid hydrogen.

### Where it is now

Pioneer 11 is on a trajectory out of the Solar System, toward the center of the galaxy. NASA says it will pass near the star Lambda Aquilae in almost four million years. NASA's latest figure is about 97.59 AU (14.599 billion km) from Earth on 5 November 2017. NASA's page does not give its current position, so the game shows it at that 2017 distance and does not extrapolate to today.

## Kepler Space Telescope: Final Card Wording

Chapter 17 · Deep space. Fact-checked against NASA Science: Kepler / K2, NASA's retirement release (30 October 2018) and NASA 3D Resources: Kepler (A).

### Intro

Kepler was NASA's first planet-hunting mission, a space telescope that searched for Earth-sized planets outside our Solar System. It launched on 6 March 2009 and became the agency's first mission to detect Earth-size planets in the habitable zones of their stars.

### Quick facts

- **Mission:** Kepler

- **Years:** 2009–2018

- **Where:** In orbit around the Sun, away from Earth

### Why it was left

In May 2013 Kepler lost the second of its four reaction wheels, which it needed to point precisely. It needed three working wheels, so the loss effectively ended new data collection for the original mission. Engineers then used the pressure of sunlight to hold its pointing, which allowed an extended mission called K2 that lasted as long as the first mission. Kepler then ran out of the fuel needed for further science operations. NASA announced on 30 October 2018 that the spacecraft was retired within its current, safe orbit, away from Earth. NASA's pages do not give the date the fuel ran out.

### The science it enabled

Kepler detected planets by watching for transits, tiny dips in a star's brightness when a planet crosses in front of it. It leaves a legacy of more than 2,600 planet discoveries from outside our Solar System. The most common planet size it found is between Earth and Neptune, a size that does not exist in our own Solar System.

### Where it is now

Kepler is in its retirement orbit around the Sun, away from Earth. NASA retired it "within its current, safe orbit, away from Earth". NASA's pages do not state its exact position or its distance from Earth, so the game's placement is approximate. The game shows it trailing Earth, as the official Space Apps challenge page describes it, but NASA's Kepler pages do not give the shape of its final orbit.
